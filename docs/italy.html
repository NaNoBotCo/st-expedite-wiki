Italy & the vanished Austro-German cult

documentedcatholic-devotionalhagiography-scholarlyitalygermanyfrancemerchants-commerceexams-studentsplace-shrine

The deep European root — Messina and Acireale in the 17th–18th centuries — and the huge German cult the 1905 Vatican action killed.

Reliability: documented. Verifiable in a primary or solid secondary record.

Before New Orleans, before Réunion, before Brazil, there was Italy — and, for a while, a German-speaking devotion so large it had a nickname.

The Italian root

Documented — The traceable cult begins in Sicily: at Messina in the 17th century, then Acireale in the 18th. On 18 April 1781 Expeditus was made the secondary patron of Acireale, patron of its merchants and navigators — the documented event whose date eerily mirrors the legendary 1781 Paris crate story. His statue stands in Acireale's Chiesa di Gesù e Maria.

Documented — Italy holds a real inventory: a Palermo parish church (built 1935, its cult approved 1899), a Bologna confraternity — the Pia Unione erected by Cardinal Svampa on 20 July 1903 with 500+ members — a 19th-century Naples cult with a "flying novena" for examinations, and some thirty statues and altars nationwide. The Palermo ten-scene vita painting above narrates his whole legend in panels.

Contested — A Turin tradition claims a street — Via Garibaldi — was called contrada di Sant'Espedito before 1437, a merchants'-patron cult that would predate everything. It needs an archival check.

"Der eilige Heilige" — the German cult that vanished

Documented — In German-speaking lands the devotion was, per folklorist Elfriede Grabner, among the most beloved folk-saint cults of the 1870s–1920s, with Vienna an early hub. He earned a nickname built on a pun — "der eilige Heilige," the saint in a hurry / the holy one in haste. The distinctive early German images show him with a clock or sundial. The best surviving witness is the statue in the Graz Franziskanerkirche, still surrounded by votive tablets and prayer cards as late as 1979. Much of the German devotion now survives only in prints — the Graz engravings and lithographs in the gallery — rather than in living parishes.

Scholarship — What killed it was Rome. The 1905 intervention under Pius X (see Controversy) fell hardest on exactly this booming, image-driven Central-European devotion.

France

Documented — France carries the devotion through churches from Bordeaux (Saint-Pierre ex-votos) to Toulouse (La Daurade; there is a Rue Saint-Expédit) to Marseille (Saint-Cannat — the very church where Réunion's cult was vowed). It also sustains a lively novena-candle trade in both Catholic and esoteric shops — and it is French scholarship, Delehaye's, that supplies the sharpest skepticism about whether the saint existed at all.
